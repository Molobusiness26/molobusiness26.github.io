How to write a career change CV

By Ink & Offer · Published October 2026

When you change career, the person reading your CV has three questions: why are you making this move, why now, and can you actually do the job? A standard CV, listing your current career in order, answers none of them. A career change CV is built to answer all three on the first page.

In this guide

  1. Pick one target role first
  2. Use a hybrid format
  3. Translate your experience into their language
  4. Find evidence beyond your job title
  5. Write the profile last
  6. A fictional example
  7. Common mistakes

Pick one target role first

The most common reason a career change CV doesn't work is that it's trying to suit several different jobs at once. "Open to opportunities in project management, operations or HR" reads as uncertainty. Pick the one role you most want, collect five or six real adverts for it, and note what they all ask for: the skills, tools, qualifications and phrases that keep coming up.

That list becomes your brief. Everything on the CV should help prove you can meet it. If you want to apply for two quite different roles, write two CVs.

Use a hybrid format

A hybrid CV combines a short skills section at the top with a normal reverse-chronological work history underneath. It's usually the best choice for career changers in the UK, because it lets you lead with what's transferable while keeping a clear, dated history that recruiters trust.

A typical order:

  1. Name and contact details.
  2. Profile: three or four lines aimed at the new role.
  3. Key skills: four to six skills taken from your target adverts, each with a short line of evidence.
  4. Relevant training or qualifications, if you've done any for the new field. Move this up when it's your strongest evidence.
  5. Work history, most recent first, with bullets chosen for relevance to the new role.
  6. Education and any other relevant sections.

A purely skills-based CV with the work history hidden at the end is sometimes recommended for career changers, but many recruiters distrust it. Keep your dates visible.

Translate your experience into their language

Every sector has its own vocabulary, and the same skill can look unrecognisable from the outside. Your job is to describe what you genuinely did in words a hiring manager in the new field will recognise, without changing the facts.

Fictional examples of translating experience for a move into project coordination.
How it's often writtenHow a project team would read it
Managed the store refitCoordinated a six-week refit with four contractors, kept to budget and reopened on schedule
Did the weekly rotaPlanned resourcing for a team of 25 across seven-day opening, balancing cover against a fixed wage budget
Dealt with head officeReported weekly progress against targets to regional and head-office stakeholders
Sorted out problemsLogged and resolved supplier and stock issues, escalating risks to the area manager

Notice that the right-hand column isn't exaggerated. It's more specific, and it uses words like coordinated, resourcing, stakeholders and risks that appear in project coordinator adverts. Only use a term if it honestly describes what you did; you'll be asked about it at interview.

Keep your real job titles. You can add a short clarifying line under a title if it's unusual, but don't rename a role to something you weren't called.

Find evidence beyond your job title

Career changers often have more relevant evidence than they realise, because it's sitting outside their main duties. Look for:

  • Projects you volunteered for at work: a new system rollout, a working group, training new starters.
  • Courses or qualifications you've started or finished for the new field.
  • Voluntary roles, side projects or freelance work in the new area.
  • Results you can quantify: time saved, money saved, satisfaction scores, errors reduced.

Put the strongest of these in your key skills section and near the top of each role, rather than at the bottom of the page.

Write the profile last

Once the rest of the CV is built, write the profile. It should say what you are now, what you're moving into, and the two strongest reasons you'll be good at it. One clear line on motivation is fine; a paragraph about passion isn't needed.

A fictional example

Fictional example

Profile (retail store manager applying for a project coordinator role at a construction firm)

"Store manager moving into project coordination, with eight years' experience running sites with 25 staff and delivering change on fixed budgets and deadlines. Coordinated two store refits and a new stock system rollout across four branches. Currently studying for an entry-level project management qualification."

Key skills

  • Planning and scheduling: built six-week refit plans covering contractors, deliveries and staff cover.
  • Stakeholder reporting: weekly progress and risk updates to area and head-office managers.
  • Budget control: managed a wage and maintenance budget, finishing each refit within its allowance.
  • Systems: Excel (scheduling, tracking), Microsoft Teams, and the retailer's stock management system.

Every line connects retail experience to something a project coordinator advert asks for. The reader doesn't have to do the translation.

Let the cover letter do the explaining

Your CV shows that you can do the job. Your cover letter explains why you want to. Two or three sentences on why you're making the move, and why this employer, are enough. See our guide to cover letters that get read for a full structure.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving the translation to the reader. They won't do it. Use their terms.
  • Hiding the dates. It raises more questions than it solves.
  • Listing every duty from your old job. Choose the ones that matter for the new one.
  • Sending the same CV everywhere. Follow our step-by-step tailoring method for each application you care about.
  • Overclaiming. If you've studied something but not used it at work, say "studying" or "trained in", not "experienced in".
