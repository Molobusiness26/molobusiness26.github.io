How to age-proof your CV, honestly

By Ink & Offer · Published October 2026

Age bias in hiring is real enough that many experienced candidates worry about it, and you can't control what a reader thinks. You can control what your CV puts in front of them. This guide shows how to keep the focus on what you offer now, without hiding anything or saying anything untrue.

What the law says, briefly

In Great Britain, the Equality Act 2010 lists age as one of nine protected characteristics. It covers job applicants as well as employees, so an employer shouldn't treat you less favourably in recruitment because of your age. Age is unusual in one respect: an employer can sometimes justify treating people differently because of age if they can show it's a proportionate way of achieving a legitimate aim. In practice that's a narrow exception, and it doesn't make general assumptions about older candidates acceptable.

The law sets the standard, but it can't stop a busy reader forming a quick impression. That's why the rest of this guide is about the CV itself. If you think you've been treated unfairly, Acas and Citizens Advice give free guidance.

Take out what was never needed

Many CVs written years ago carry details that a UK CV simply doesn't need today. Removing them isn't concealment; it's current practice, and it gives your best material more room.

  • Date of birth and age. Not needed. Employers who need to confirm your age for a legal reason will ask separately.
  • Photo. Not expected on a UK CV.
  • Marital status, children, nationality. Not needed. If the advert asks about the right to work in the UK, answer that question directly.
  • Full home address. Your town or area is enough.
  • "References available on request". Employers assume it.

Two things that may show your date of birth later are entirely normal. Equality monitoring forms are optional to complete and are normally kept separate from the people deciding on your application. And UK employers must check your right to work before you start, which can mean showing a passport or another document that includes your date of birth. That usually happens at offer stage.

Handle dates honestly

This is where advice often goes too far. Removing every date can make a CV look as if something is being held back, and many applicant tracking systems use employment dates to work out your experience. Changing dates to make yourself look younger is dishonest and can cost you an offer if it comes to light. A better approach keeps the CV accurate and still puts the emphasis where you want it.

  • Show dates on your recent roles. Years alone are fine ("2016 to 2025"). Be consistent.
  • Summarise earlier roles. A short "Earlier career" section can cover the first part of your working life in one or two lines, without a bullet list for every job. Our guide to writing a CV when you're over 50 shows how.
  • Graduation and school dates are optional. Name the qualification and where you gained it. Include the year if it's recent, or if the advert asks.
  • Don't hide gaps; explain them. One honest line is enough. See how to write a CV after a career break.

Whatever you leave out of the CV, be ready to talk through your full history at interview. The aim is a document that is selective, not one that is misleading.

Check the language, not just the dates

Some phrases signal the length of a career before the reader has looked at a single date. They aren't wrong, but they spend your opening on a number instead of on what you can do.

  • "Over 30 years' experience in…" Lead with the type of role and a result instead. The depth will show in your work history.
  • "Seeking a new challenge to see me through to retirement". Say what you'll bring to this role, not where it fits in your life plan.
  • Old job titles and system names. If your title has a modern equivalent, you can add it in brackets, as long as it fairly describes the job you did. Use the current name of software, or say which version you've used recently.
  • Long lists of duties. Three or four achievements with scale (team size, budget, number of customers) read as more energetic than a page of responsibilities.

Fictional example

Before

"Loyal and experienced professional with over 35 years in purchasing, looking for a final role where my experience will be valued."

After (for a procurement manager role)

"Procurement manager who runs supplier tenders for a £12m annual spend. Renegotiated three major contracts in the last two years, using e-sourcing tools and monthly supplier scorecards to cut delivery failures."

The "after" version is about the job being applied for, uses present-tense evidence and names current tools. Nothing in it is invented.

Show that your skills are current

The unspoken question many readers have about an experienced candidate is whether they're up to date. Answer it with specifics rather than claims.

  • Name the systems you use now: the finance package, the CRM, the stock system, the video-call and collaboration tools your team works in.
  • Include recent learning: a short course, a refreshed qualification, a new certification, or training others on a new process.
  • Show recent change you've led or adapted to, such as a system migration, a move to hybrid working or a new regulation.
  • If you have a LinkedIn profile, make sure it matches your CV and has a recent, natural photo. A professional email address based on your name helps too.

Make the layout look like today

A CV's appearance can date it as quickly as its content. A clean, single-column layout, a standard font at a comfortable size, clear headings and plenty of white space all help. Two pages is normal for an experienced UK CV. Avoid text boxes, tables and graphics in the main body, because some application systems read them poorly; our guide to ATS-friendly CVs explains why. Send Word or PDF, depending on what the employer asks for.

What not to do

  • Don't change or invent dates. Being selective is fine; being inaccurate isn't.
  • Don't apologise for your age or refer to it in the CV or cover letter. It isn't relevant, so leave it out.
  • Don't undersell yourself by cutting real achievements just because they were senior. If you're choosing a less senior role on purpose, say so briefly in your cover letter.
  • Don't send one CV to every job. Tailoring to each advert matters more than any formatting trick. See how to tailor your CV to a job advert.

A quick checklist

  • No date of birth, photo, marital status or full address.
  • Dates shown consistently on recent roles; earlier career summarised.
  • A profile that leads with the role and a result, not years of service.
  • Current system names and recent learning near the top.
  • Achievements with scale, not lists of duties.
  • A clean, two-page, single-column layout.
  • Everything accurate, and ready to discuss at interview.
